1. Channels are partitions; the lattice data-processing law

A deterministic feedback channel on records \(\{0,1\}^n\) is a function \(f\); two channels carry the same information exactly when \(\ker f\) agrees, so a channel is a partition of the record set. Convention: \(p\le q\) when \(q\) refines \(p\); the one-block partition (sealed) is the bottom, the discrete partition (item_level) the top, \(p\vee q\) the common refinement (observing both) and \(p\wedge q\) the finest common coarsening (components of the union of the two relations). This is the partition lattice, orientation reversed.

Proposition 1 (lattice data-processing law and converse). For all distributions \(P,Q\) on records, \(p\le q\) implies \(\mathrm{TV}(p_\#P,p_\#Q)\le\mathrm{TV}(q_\#P,q_\#Q)\); and if \(p\not\le q\) there are point masses \(P,Q\) reversing the inequality.

Proof. \(\mathrm{TV}(p_\#P,p_\#Q)=\max_U|P(U)-Q(U)|\) over unions \(U\) of blocks of \(p\). When \(q\) refines \(p\), every block of \(p\) is a union of blocks of \(q\), so the maximum for \(p\) ranges over a subset of the sets available to \(q\). If \(q\) does not refine \(p\), some block of \(q\) holds \(x,y\) that \(p\) separates; \(P=\delta_x,Q=\delta_y\) give \(\mathrm{TV}\) one through \(p\) and zero through \(q\). \(\square\)

So \(\mathrm{TV}\) through \(p\vee q\) is at least the larger of the two and through \(p\wedge q\) at most the smaller; the testbed's one-kernel contraction is one comparable pair. Evidence status: algebraic identity. Fixture lattice_fixture(2, 2): all 15 partitions of \(\{0,1\}^2\), 225 ordered pairs, 100 pairs of grid distributions; the law holds on every comparable pair, fails somewhere on every non-comparable pair, join and meet satisfy their lattice definitions, and access_kernel's three classes are bottom, middle, top.

2. Category leakage is an order embedding, not a scale

A category map, a partition \(\gamma\) of the items \([n]\), induces the channel \(\Phi(\gamma)\): the per-category pass-count vector.

Proposition 2. \(\Phi(\gamma)\le\Phi(\gamma')\) iff \(\gamma\le\gamma'\), so \(\Phi\) is an order embedding; \(\Phi(\text{one block})=\)score_only and \(\Phi(\text{singletons})=\)item_level.

Proof. If \(\gamma'\) refines \(\gamma\), each \(\gamma\)-count is a sum of \(\gamma'\)-counts, so equal \(\gamma'\)-counts force equal \(\gamma\)-counts. If not, some \(\gamma'\)-block holds items \(i,j\) that \(\gamma\) separates; the records failing only \(i\) and only \(j\) have equal \(\gamma'\)-counts and unequal \(\gamma\)-counts. The endpoints are the definitions. \(\square\) Evidence status: algebraic identity, checked exhaustively on all 5 maps of \([3]\) and 15 maps of \([4]\) in category_order_fixture.

The image is order-isomorphic to the partition lattice of \([n]\), not a chain for \(n\ge3\), so "how much leaks" has no scalar. On \([3]\), with \(A=\{0,1\}|\{2\}\) and \(B=\{0\}|\{1,2\}\): \(B\) separates "fail item 0" from "fail item 1" (\(\mathrm{TV}=1\)) while \(A\) gives 0, and conversely for "fail 1" vs "fail 2".

Proposition 2b (meets are preserved). \(\Phi(\gamma\wedge\gamma')= \Phi(\gamma)\wedge\Phi(\gamma')\) for all \(\gamma,\gamma'\).

Proof. \(\Phi\) is an order embedding, so \(\Phi(\gamma\wedge\gamma')\le \Phi(\gamma)\wedge\Phi(\gamma')\). Conversely let \(r,s\) have equal pass counts on every block of \(\gamma\wedge\gamma'\). Those blocks are the connected components of the graph on items whose edges join two items lying in a common \(\gamma\)-block or a common \(\gamma'\)-block. Swapping the bits at two items of one \(\gamma\)-block preserves every \(\gamma\)-count, so a record and its swap share a \(\Phi(\gamma)\)-block, hence a block of the meet; likewise for \(\gamma'\). The edge transpositions of a connected graph generate its symmetric group, so within each component every arrangement of a fixed number of passes is reached from every other by such swaps. Hence \(r\) and \(s\) share a block of the meet. \(\square\) Evidence status: algebraic identity, checked on all 25, 225 and 2704 ordered pairs of maps on 3, 4 and 5 items (category_lattice_fixture).

Proposition 2c (joins are preserved iff the intersection graph is a forest). Let \(I(\gamma,\gamma')\) be the bipartite graph on the blocks of \(\gamma\) and of \(\gamma'\) with an edge where two blocks intersect; its edges are the cells of \(\gamma\vee\gamma'\). Then \(\Phi(\gamma)\vee\Phi(\gamma')=\Phi(\gamma\vee\gamma')\) iff \(I(\gamma,\gamma')\) is acyclic.

Proof. \(\Phi(\gamma\vee\gamma')\) identifies records with equal cell counts, \(\Phi(\gamma)\vee\Phi(\gamma')\) those with equal row and column margins; cell counts determine margins, so the refined channel is always at least as fine. If \(I\) has a cycle \(A_1B_1A_2B_2\cdots A_kB_k\), put one pass in each cell \(A_i\cap B_i\) for one record and one in each \(A_{i+1}\cap B_i\) for the other: every block on the cycle then holds exactly one pass in both records, so the margins agree while the cells differ. If \(I\) is a forest, peel leaves: a leaf block has a single nonempty cell, whose count is that block's margin; remove it and recurse, so the margins determine every cell. \(\square\)

For rows \(\{0,1\}|\{2,3\}\) and columns \(\{0,2\}|\{1,3\}\) the graph is a 4-cycle and 1001, 0110 is the witness: two separate category leaks are strictly coarser than one leak with the refined categories. On \([4]\) the six failing ordered pairs are the three pairings into two pairs taken crosswise; on \([5]\) 210 of 2704 ordered pairs fail, and join_failure_witness builds a valid witness from a cycle for each. Evidence status: algebraic identity, checked exhaustively on 3, 4, 5 items.

3. Cost of a leak, exact

Setting of noise_ceiling_fixture: clean accuracy \(4/5\), flip \(1/10\), so \(v=37/50\), ceiling \(9/10\); on \(n=50\) items 13 fail and \(H=8\) hits are needed. Under \(\Phi(\gamma)\) the loop knows \((n_c,F_c)\) per category and patches uniformly at random without replacement; a patch on a passed item gains nothing.

Proposition 3 (expected gain is exact; greedy is optimal). The \(j\)-th patch in category \(c\) hits with conditional probability \((F_c-h)/(n_c-j+1)\) given \(h\) earlier hits, and with unconditional probability \(F_c/n_c\) for every \(j\) (exchangeability). So \(m_c\) patches hit exactly \(m_cF_c/n_c\) in expectation: the expected-value formula of the insertions note is the hypergeometric mean, not an approximation. Every patch in \(c\) has the same marginal value, so filling categories in decreasing order of \(F_c/n_c\) maximizes expected hits at every patch count. Algebraic identity; checked against every allocation and every patch order.

Proposition 4 (patches to the ceiling). With \(c_1,c_2,\dots\) the greedy order, \(j\) the first index with \(\sum_{i\le j}F_{c_i}\ge H\), \(N_{<j}=\sum_{i<j}n_{c_i}\) and \(H_j=H-\sum_{i<j}F_{c_i}\in[1,F_{c_j}]\): the expected score reaches the ceiling at \(m^*=N_{<j}+H_jn_{c_j}/F_{c_j}\), and the realized score first reaches it at a stopping time \(T\) with \(\mathbb E[T]=N_{<j}+H_j(n_{c_j}+1)/(F_{c_j}+1)\) (negative-hypergeometric mean; earlier categories are patched completely, so their hits are fixed). Hence \(m^*-\mathbb E[T]=H_j(n_{c_j}-F_{c_j})/(F_{c_j}(F_{c_j}+1))\in[0,m^*/(F_{c_j}+1))\): the expected-score crossing never understates the expected patch count. One category: \(m^*/n=(1-\varepsilon-v)/(1-v)=8/13\), \(\mathbb E[T]=204/7\). Singleton categories: both equal \(H\), share \(4/25\). Algebraic identity, cross-checked by enumerating every patch order on three category shapes and against ceiling_detection_threshold and targeted_ceiling_detection_threshold.

Proposition 5 (monotonicity of \(m^*\)). If \(\gamma'\) refines \(\gamma\) then \(m^*(\gamma')\le m^*(\gamma)\) for every failure pattern. Proof. Each \(\gamma\)-rate is the size-weighted mean of its \(\gamma'\)-sub-rates, so the \(\gamma\) expected-hit curve is attainable under \(\gamma'\) by proportional (fractional) allocation, and the \(\gamma'\)-greedy curve is at least it. \(\square\) Algebraic identity; checked over all 203 maps of \([6]\), 2268 refining pairs, 63 patterns, two requirements each.

The stopping time under the static schedule is not monotone. \(\mathbb E[T]\) under the static greedy schedule can rise along refinement: one block \((4,2)\), \(H=2\), gives \(10/3\); \((2,1),(2,1)\) gives \(7/2\). On \([6]\): record 001101, \(H=3\), \(21/4\) against \(11/2\). Finite verified example, a proof by witness.

Proposition 6 (the optimal adaptive schedule is monotone). Let \(V^*(\gamma)\) be the minimum, over adaptive policies that re-query the channel after each patch and patch a uniformly random unpatched item of a category they choose, of the expected patches to \(H\) hits. If \(\gamma'\) refines \(\gamma\) then \(V^*(\gamma')\le V^*(\gamma)\).

Proof. A \(\gamma\)-policy's information, the coarse counts, is a function of the \(\gamma'\)-information, and patching a uniformly random unpatched item of a coarse block is the same as choosing a sub-block with probability proportional to its unpatched items and patching at random there. So every \(\gamma\)-policy is a \(\gamma'\)-policy with the same law of patch outcomes, and the minimum over \(\gamma'\)-policies is no larger. \(\square\) optimal_adaptive_value computes \(V^*\) by dynamic programming on the multiset of (remaining, failures) pairs. Evidence status: algebraic identity; checked on all 2268 refining pairs of \([6]\), 162 cases each (greedy_vs_optimal_fixture).

Theorem 10 (one hit: the best finisher, in closed form). With one hit needed the optimal expected patch count is the minimum of \((n_c+1)/(F_c+1)\) over the categories with \(F_c\ge1\), attained by patching a category of minimal \((n_c+1)/(F_c+1)\) until it hits and never switching. Proof. Induct on the total number of items. Sticking with \(c\) costs \(1+(1-F_c/n_c)\,n_c/(F_c+1)=(n_c+1)/(F_c+1)\). A miss in \(c\) lowers its value to \(n_c/(F_c+1)\) and changes nothing else, so if \(c\) was a best finisher it still is, and by induction continuing with it is optimal from the new state. A first patch in a category without failures is a sure miss that leaves every finisher unchanged, so it costs \(1+v>v\) with \(v\) the best finisher's value. Starting in any other \(c'\), now with \(F_{c'}\ge1\), costs at least \(1+(1-F_{c'}/n_{c'})\min(n_{c'}/(F_{c'}+1),\,v)\); the first branch gives \((n_{c'}+1)/(F_{c'}+1)\ge v\), and the second gives \(1+(1-F_{c'}/n_{c'})v\ge v\) because \(v\le(n_{c'}+1)/(F_{c'}+1)\le n_{c'}/F_{c'}\), which needs \(F_{c'}\ge1\). \(\square\) Algebraic identity; one_hit_fixture checks the closed form against the dynamic programme on every state with at most four categories of size at most eight. Correction in version 0.7 (found by the Lean formalization): versions 0.5 and 0.6 wrote the minimum over all categories, which is wrong when one has no failure: \(\{(1,0),(10,1)\}\) costs \(11/2\), while \((1+1)/(0+1)=2\). one_hit_value always took the minimum over categories with failures, so only the statement changes; the receipt now carries this state as a guard. The highest current rate is therefore not the right rule at one hit: in \(\{(2,1),(16,9)\}\) the rates are \(\tfrac12<\tfrac9{16}\) but the finishers are \(\tfrac32<\tfrac{17}{10}\), and patching \((2,1)\) first costs \(3/2\) against \(53/32\) for the higher rate. The exchange argument that version 0.2 offered for "greedy is optimal at one hit" only compares policies that would patch the other category next; it does not cover a policy that stays in a category whose rate rose after a miss. On the exhaustive grid the two rules happen to agree, which is why the error survived the checks.

Greedy is not optimal. The greedy rule (highest current rate, ties to the smaller category) is one adaptive policy, so \(V^*\le\) greedy \(\le\) static everywhere. It agrees with the optimum at one hit on the grid, but not in general (Theorem 10's witness), and it is optimal when every failure must be hit, since any policy that never patches an exhausted category needs \(\sum_cF_c(n_c+1)/(F_c+1)\) patches, the sum of the last-failure positions. It is not optimal in general: with categories \((4,2)\) and \((2,1)\) and two hits needed, greedy patches the smaller category first and needs \(19/6\) patches; patching the larger first needs \(37/12\). On six items greedy is strictly worse in 180 of 32,886 cases; on four and five items it is optimal in every case. The greedy value was monotone on every enumerated refining pair, which Proposition 6 does not imply; what it implies is that the right cost of a leak, the optimal expected stopping time, is monotone everywhere. Chain with block sizes 50, 25, 10, 5, 2, 1 and failures at items \(0,4,\dots,48\): \(m^*/n=8/13,7/12,8/15,1/2,8/25,4/25\); \(\mathbb E[T]_{\text{static}}=204/7,201/7,51/2,23,31/2,8\); greedy adaptive \(\approx29.14,25.64,21.17,18,12,8\).

Proposition 7 (no index rule is optimal). An index rule scores each category by a function of its own \((n_c,F_c)\), the requirement \(H\), and possibly global summaries (total items, total failures, number of categories), and patches a maximizer, ties broken by a fixed order on those same inputs; it therefore ranks two categories the same way in any two states that agree on those inputs. No such rule is optimal. With \(H=2\), \((2,1)\) is the unique optimal first patch of \(\{(2,1),(2,1),(4,2)\}\) (\(3\) against \(37/12\)) and \((4,2)\) the unique optimal first patch of \(\{(2,1),(4,2)\}\) (\(37/12\) against \(19/6\)); an optimal index rule would have to rank \((2,1)\) above \((4,2)\) in the first state and below it in the second. With the totals and the category count added to the index, \((2,1)\) is uniquely optimal in \(\{(2,1),(2,1),(4,1),(4,2)\}\) (at \(3\)) and \((4,2)\) in \(\{(2,1),(3,1),(3,1),(4,2)\}\) (at \(37/12\)), both with twelve items, five failures, four categories and \(H=2\). Proof. By witness; the values are exact dynamic-programme continuations (first_patch_values, optimal_first_choices). \(\square\) A reversal, \(A\) before \(B\) strictly better in one state and strictly worse in another, refutes an index rule only when the preferred category is the unique optimum in both states: otherwise a third category can tie for the optimum and a rule that ranks it first is optimal there. preference_reversals finds 30 reversals for own-count indices over all 33,915 states with at most four categories of size at most five, and 125 and 120 with the two richer contexts; of these, 8, 18 and 12 are refuting pairs, with the preferred category the unique optimum on both sides. Evidence status: algebraic identity (a counterexample proves the universal negative); the counts are a finite verified example. Correction in version 0.7 (found by the Lean formalization): version 0.6's witnesses did not refute. In \(\{(1,1),(2,1),(4,2)\}\) with \(H=2\) the first-patch values are \(5/2\), \(5/2\), \(8/3\), so \((1,1)\) is optimal too, and in the totals witness \(\{(1,1),(2,1),(3,1),(4,2)\}\) with \(H=3\), \((1,1)\) and \((4,2)\) tie at \(49/12\); its counts of 30, 125 and 120 counted reversals, not refutations. The proposition stands on the witnesses above; Theorem 11 explains them at two hits.

Proposition 8 (the optimal first patch need not have the highest hit probability). In \(\{(2,1),(5,2)\}\) with \(H=2\) the rates are \(\tfrac12\) and \(\tfrac25\); patching \((5,2)\) first needs \(69/20\) patches, \((2,1)\) first \(7/2\). So beyond \(H=1\) the optimal policy is not "a highest-rate category with the right tie-break": rate_violations finds 82 states among the 33,915 in which every optimal first patch has a strictly sub-maximal rate, none with \(H=1\) on that grid, where the highest-rate and best-finisher orders never strictly disagree (they do at larger sizes, Theorem 10). The same enumeration puts greedy's first patch off the optimum in 131 states, and no \(H\)-dependent tie-break repairs the 82. Evidence status: algebraic identity for the witness; finite verified example for the counts. What remains open is a structural description of the optimal policy; the dynamic programme is exact and cheap on these grids, so the gap is one of understanding (version 0.7: closed for two hits by Theorem 11; for three or more the programme's state count is exponential in the number of categories, see the complexity paragraph).

Lemma 9 (pair commutation). With at least two hits still needed, patching category \(A\) and then \(B\ne A\), each followed by an optimal continuation, costs the same in expectation as \(B\) and then \(A\). Proof. Patches in different categories have independent outcomes, and the state after both is the same multiset whichever came first; the run cannot stop before the second patch, since two hits were needed; and the continuation is a function of the state. \(\square\) Algebraic identity; checked on all 114,835 ordered pairs over the 33,915 states (pair_commutation_check). So the order of the next two patches is immaterial unless one of them could finish the run, which is why the exchange argument settles \(H=1\) and nothing beyond it; Theorem 11 settles \(H=2\) by a different route (Lemma 12).

What the enumeration rules out (policy_structure_fixture, sizes at most five, at most four categories). No extremal rule covers the optimal first patch: in 54 states it is none of the highest-rate, the largest, the best-finisher (smallest \((n+1)/(F+1)\)) or the worst-finisher category. Equal rates do not favour the larger category: in \(\{(2,1),(2,1),(4,2)\}\) with two hits needed the optimal first patch is a \((2,1)\), against the two-category witness of Proposition 7, in four such states. Two regularities survived every state of that grid and fail just beyond it (conjecture_refutations): with exactly two categories of equal rate the larger is not always optimal first for \(H\ge2\), since in \(\{(3,2),(6,4)\}\) with two hits needed the smaller costs \(13/5\) against \(121/45\) (58 such pairs among 1,044 with sizes at most twelve); and the lowest of three or more distinct rates can be the unique optimal first patch, as in \(\{(5,3),(8,5),(9,6)\}\) with three hits needed, where the rate-\(\tfrac35\) category costs \(21/5\) against \(1675/392\) and \(443/105\). Each refutation is an exact state; neither regularity is a theorem, and the earlier conjecture is withdrawn.

Committing until a hit. Theorem 10 suggests one more candidate shape: commit to a category until it hits, then re-decide, never switching on a miss. That class contains the one-hit optimum and is exact there, and it is not optimal in general: in \(\{(4,2),(4,2),(6,3)\}\) with four hits needed the best committing policy costs \(509/80\) against the optimum \(763/120\), and commit_until_hit_fixture finds 82 such states among the 39,060 with at most three categories of size at most seven, all with four to eleven hits needed. The gap is small everywhere on that grid, below one part in a thousand (one part in 1,526 at most), so an optimal policy does switch categories after a miss, and gains almost nothing by it. Finite verified example. Version 0.7 sharpens all three clauses: the class is exact at two hits as well (Theorem 11), fails at three and at every larger requirement (Corollary 14), and its gap exceeds one part in a thousand one size class beyond this grid (below).

Two hits in closed form; where committing stops (version 0.7)

Write \(g(n,F)=(n+1)/(F+1)\) for the finisher of a category with \(F\ge1\). Patched in uniformly random order, its first failure sits at \(T\) with \(P(T=t)=\binom{n-t}{F-1}/\binom nF\) for \(t=1,\dots,n-F+1\) (first_hit_law); \(\mathbb E[T]=g(n,F)\). After a first hit at \(T\) the category is \((n-T,F-1)\), with finisher \(\varphi(T)=(n-T+1)/F\) (\(+\infty\) when \(F=1\)), and \(\mathbb E[\varphi(T)]=g(n,F)\): the finisher has the same mean before and after a commitment. \(K_c(s)\) is the cost of committing to \(c\) until it hits and playing optimally afterwards (commit_first_value); \(V^*_h\) is the optimum with \(h\) hits needed; in a state \(s\), \(c'\) is \(c\) after a miss, \(c^*\) is \(c\) after a hit, and \(s'\), \(s^{\rm hit}_c\) the corresponding states.

Theorem 11 (two hits: commit until the first hit). With two hits needed, \[ V^*_2=\min_c\kappa(n_c,F_c,M_{-c}),\qquad \kappa(n,F,M)=\frac{n+1}{F+1}+\mathbb E\bigl[\min(\varphi(T),M)\bigr], \] the minimum over categories with \(F_c\ge1\), where \(M_{-c}\) is the best finisher among the other categories with failures (\(+\infty\) if there is none). The optimal first patches are exactly the minimizers: an optimal policy commits to one until it hits, then commits to the best finisher.

Proof. \(\kappa(n_c,F_c,M_{-c})=K_c(s)\), by Theorem 10 after the hit, so \(V^*_2\le\min_cK_c\). Induct on the total number of items. Let \(c\) be an optimal first patch with \(p=F_c/n_c\). If \(F_c=n_c\) the patch is a sure hit, a commitment of length one. Otherwise, by induction \(V^*_2(s')=\min_eK_e(s')\). If \(c'\) attains it, \(V^*_2(s)=1+pV^*_1(s^{\rm hit}_c)+(1-p)K_{c'}(s')=K_c(s)\). If instead \(K_d(s')<K_{c'}(s')\) for some \(d\ne c\), then \(V^*_2(s)=1+pV^*_1(s^{\rm hit}_c)+(1-p)K_d(s')\), and three facts give a contradiction: (i) \(K_d(s)-K_d(s')=\mathbb E[\min(g_c,Z)-\min(g_{c'},Z)]\le g_c-g_{c'}=1/(F_c+1)\), with \(Z\) the best other finisher after \(d\)'s first hit; (ii) \(K_{c'}(s')\le g_{c'}+V^*_1(s^{\rm hit}_c)\), because \(c'\) after its first hit has the failures of \(c^*\) and fewer items, hence a smaller finisher; (iii) \(p\,g_{c'}=F_c/(F_c+1)\). So \[ V^*_2(s)-K_d(s)=1+pV^*_1(s^{\rm hit}_c)-pK_d(s')-\bigl(K_d(s)-K_d(s')\bigr) >1-p\,g_{c'}-\tfrac1{F_c+1}=0, \] against \(V^*_2(s)\le K_d(s)\). Hence \(V^*_2(s)=K_c(s)\) for every optimal \(c\), and every minimizer of \(K\) is optimal because \(K_c\ge V^*_2\). \(\square\)

With \(r=n-T\) the items left after the first hit, \(P(r)=\binom r{F-1}/\binom nF\), and the hockey-stick identities give \(\mathbb E[\min(\varphi,M)]=\bigl(\binom{R+2}{F+1}+M(\binom nF-\binom{R+1}F)\bigr)/\binom nF\) with \(R\) the largest \(r\le n-1\) such that \((r+1)/F\le M\) (two_hit_score_closed_form). So two_hit_value needs one pass over the categories and one first-hit law each: time linear in the number of items, where the dynamic programme's state count is exponential in the number of categories. Evidence status: algebraic identity. The receipt checks the value, the set of optimal first patches and the committing value against the programme on all 4,487 two-hit states with at most three categories of size at most seven, three larger states, and the closed form on 3,186 cases; one-off exact runs of two_hit_fixture(10, 4) and two_hit_fixture(16, 3) agree on all 455,115 and 437,972 states.

At two hits the optimal first patch is therefore an index rule once the index may see the two smallest finishers: \(M_{-c}\) is the smallest finisher overall unless \(c\) is the only category attaining it, and then the second smallest. Proposition 7's contexts do not contain them, which is why rules on those contexts fail. The corrected witness reads off directly: in \(\{(2,1),(4,2)\}\) the scores are \(19/6\) and \(37/12\); a second \((2,1)\) lowers the first one's best other finisher from \(5/3\) to \(3/2\) and its score to \(3\).

Lemma 12 (the price of one switch). For any requirement \(h\ge1\), any state \(s\), a category \(c=(n,F)\) with \(F<n\), \(p=F/n\), and another category \(d\): let \(\Pi\) be the policy that patches \(c\) once, plays optimally after a hit, and after a miss commits to \(d\) until it hits and then plays optimally. Then \[ \Pi-K_d(s)=\tfrac1{F+1}+p\bigl(K_{c'}(s')-K_d(s')\bigr)+p\,\mathbb E[\rho]-\mathbb E[\delta],\qquad \Pi-K_c(s)=(1-p)\bigl(K_d(s')-K_{c'}(s')\bigr), \] where \(\delta=V^*_{h-1}(c,W,\dots)-V^*_{h-1}(c',W,\dots)\), averaged over \(d\)'s state \(W\) after its first hit, is what one pass of \(c\) is worth once \(d\) has hit, and \(\rho=V^*_{h-1}(c^*,d,\dots)-V^*_{h-1}(c^*\text{ less }T'\text{ passes},d,\dots)\), averaged over the first-failure position \(T'\) of \(c'\), is what the passes that \(c'\) misses before its first hit are worth once \(c\) has hit. Proof. Committing to \(c\) is patching it once and committing to \(c'\) after a miss, which gives the second line. For the first, \(K_d(s)-K_d(s')=\mathbb E[\delta]\) by definition, \(K_{c'}(s')=g_{c'}+V^*_{h-1}(s^{\rm hit}_c)-\mathbb E[\rho]\) because \(c'\) after its first hit is \(c^*\) less \(T'\) passes, and \(p\,g_{c'}=F/(F+1)\); substitute into \(\Pi-K_d(s)=1+pV^*_{h-1}(s^{\rm hit}_c)-pK_d(s')-(K_d(s)-K_d(s'))\). \(\square\) Algebraic identity; switch_after_miss checks it exactly on all 15,650 ordered triples \((s,c,d)\) with at most three categories of size at most five.

Call \(\sigma=1/(F+1)+p\,\mathbb E[\rho]-\mathbb E[\delta]\) the slack. If \(\sigma\ge0\), \(\Pi\ge\min(K_c(s),K_d(s))\): when \(K_{c'}(s')\ge K_d(s')\) the first line gives \(\Pi\ge K_d(s)\), otherwise the second gives \(\Pi>K_c(s)\). So patching \(c\) once and switching after a miss beats both commitments only if \(\sigma<0\): one pass of \(c\), after \(d\)'s hit, must be worth more than \(1/(F+1)\), which is what a pass is worth to \(c\)'s next hit alone. Theorem 11's step is the case \(h=2\), where \(\delta\le1/(F+1)\) and \(\rho\ge0\). Beyond two hits the slack can be negative without a profitable switch (\(-1/12\) in \(\{(3,1),(4,2),(5,2)\}\) with three hits, \(c=(4,2)\), \(d=(3,1)\)).

Corollary (one failure). If \(F=1\) then \(\delta\le1/2\), so \(\sigma\ge0\). Proof. Make \(c\) from \(c'\) by marking one of \(c\)'s passes, uniformly at random, as extra; a policy for \(c\) can mark online, declaring each newly seen pass extra with probability one over the number of \(c\)'s passes not yet seen, and so run an optimal policy for the state with \(c'\), paying one more patch exactly when the extra pass lies among the items it patches. The extra pass is equally likely to sit in each of the \(n\) slots around \(c'\)'s items, independently of \(c'\)'s order, so the expected extra cost is the expected number of \(c'\)-items patched over \(n\), at most \(\mathbb E[T_{c'}]/n=1/2\), since \(c'\) has one failure and is spent at its hit. \(\square\) When every category has one failure, committing is therefore optimal for every requirement, by Theorem 11's induction with \(\sigma\ge0\) in place of facts (i)–(iii), and each commitment spends its category at expected cost \((n_c+1)/2\) and leaves the others unchanged, so \(V^*_H\) is the sum of the \(H\) smallest \((n_c+1)/2\) (single_failure_value). Algebraic identity; the receipt checks \(\delta\le1/2\) on all 5,300 single-failure rows and the closed form on all 1,760 single-failure states with at most four categories of size at most eight.

Lemma 13 (a sure hit goes first). For any state \(s\) and \(h\ge1\), \(V^*_h(s\cup\{(1,1)\})=1+V^*_{h-1}(s)\), and the same holds for the best committing value. Proof. Patching \((1,1)\) first gives \(\le\). From any policy for \(s\cup\{(1,1)\}\), delete its patch of \((1,1)\) if it makes one and stop as soon as \(h-1\) hits are in: this is a policy for \(s\) (committing if the original was), and on every path it is at least one patch shorter, since either the deleted patch was one of the original's hits or the original's \((h-1)\)-th hit came at least one patch before its \(h\)-th. \(\square\) Algebraic identity; checked on the padded states below.

Corollary 14 (where committing is exact). Committing until a hit is optimal at every state exactly when at most two hits are needed. Proof. Theorems 10 and 11 for \(H\le2\). For \(H=3\), \(\{(4,2),(5,2),(14,7)\}\) has \(V^*=1055429/205920\) and best committing value \(351823/68640\), a gap of \(1/5148\) (one part in 26,386); the optimum patches \((4,2)\) and after a miss switches to \((14,7)\) (\(46727/10296<15577/3432\)), and Lemma 12's slack there is \(-2291/205920\) (\(\mathbb E[\delta]=6157/10296\), \(\mathbb E[\rho]=5801/11440\)). Lemma 13 with \(H-3\) copies of \((1,1)\) carries the same gap to every \(H\ge3\). \(\square\) Algebraic identity (witness plus padding, checked for \(H=4,5,6\) in commit_boundary_fixture). The three-hit failure needs size fourteen: a one-off exact scan of all 204,023 three-hit states with at most three categories of size at most fourteen finds this one state and no other, and none among the 91,338 with at most four categories of size at most eight. It is another regularity that held on every smaller grid and failed one size beyond.

The complexity question, stated. PATCH: given categories \((n_c,F_c)\), \(1\le F_c\le n_c\), with sizes in unary, a requirement \(1\le H\le\sum_cF_c\), and a rational \(\theta\) in binary, is \(V^*_H\le\theta\)? The input length is of order \(\sum_cn_c\) plus the bits of \(\theta\). What is known (algebraic identities, by counting and the results above): (i) the dynamic programme on per-category progress has at most \(\prod_c(n_c+1)(F_c+1)\) states, each solved with \(O(C)\) work, so PATCH is polynomial for every fixed number \(C\) of categories; on the multiset of remaining pairs it has at most \(\binom{C+T}T\) states with \(T=N(N+1)/2\) and \(N=\max_cn_c\), so it is polynomial for every fixed maximum size. A hardness proof must let both grow. (ii) \(H=1\) takes \(O(C)\) time (Theorem 10), \(H=2\) time linear in the item count (Theorem 11), \(H=\sum_cF_c\) is the sum of last-failure positions, and instances with one failure per category are a sort (Corollary to Lemma 12). (iii) The best committing value is polynomial for every fixed \(H\): at its decision times at most \(H-1\) categories have been touched, each in at most \((N+1)^2\) states, so it visits at most \(\sum_{k<H}C^k(N+1)^{2k}\) states with \(O(CN)\) work each; it equals \(V^*\) at every state for \(H\le2\) and, for each \(H\ge3\), not at every state. (iv) Open: whether \(V^*_3\) is polynomial (after Theorem 11 the three-hit problem is a single path to the first hit with explicit costs after it, but the witness's path already uses two categories), and whether PATCH is hard when \(H\) is part of the input. No reduction was attempted; nothing here suggests NP-, #P- or PSPACE-hardness, and nothing rules it out.

How far committing is from the optimum. Finite verified example only; no bound is proved. Version 0.6's "below one part in a thousand" held on its grid and fails beyond it: \(\{(10,2),(12,2),(19,3)\}\) with four hits needed has \(V^*=7477604/479655\approx15.590\) and best committing value \(22463159/1438965\approx15.611\), a relative gap of \(30347/22432812\), one part in 739 (commit_boundary_fixture). A one-off exact run of commit_until_hit_fixture(24, 3, max_failures=3) finds it the largest among all 347,616 states with at most three categories of at most three failures and size at most 24, of which 2,006 have a gap, all with four to six hits needed. Doubling every size, \(\{(20,2),(24,2),(38,3)\}\), gives \(4311523/4400921494\), one part in 1,021 (one-off exact run), so the gap does not simply grow with size; whether it is bounded at all is open.

Two categories. Finite verified example; not a theorem. With two categories, committing is optimal and every optimal first patch stays optimal after a miss in it, on all 782,600 two-category states with sizes at most 24 (one-off exact run of two_category_commit_fixture(24); the receipt covers size eight), and Lemma 12's slack is nonnegative for both orders on all 59,360 states with sizes at most fourteen (98,735 slacks). Theorem 11's argument would extend to every requirement given exactly that inequality, \(\mathbb E[\delta]\le1/(F+1)+p\,\mathbb E[\rho]\), for two categories; it is false with three. With two hits, two categories are covered by Theorem 11. A switching curve is therefore a curve between two commitments, but it is not characterized here.

At most two failures per category. A stressed regularity, not a theorem. Committing is optimal on every state checked in which each category has at most two failures: one-off exact runs of commit_until_hit_fixture with max_failures=2 over all 391,878 states with at most three categories of size at most 40, 715,778 with four of size at most twenty, 696,150 with five of size at most twelve and 447,678 with six of size at most eight; the receipt covers four categories of size at most eight. Every committing gap found has a category with at least three failures. Only the one-failure case is proved (Corollary to Lemma 12); the two-failure case can have negative slack, as in the example above, so a proof needs more than Lemma 12.

4. Ledger wrapper

audit_category_ledger accepts a category_feedback event carrying the partition used, records it with its position (score_only, category, item_level), and forwards to audit_ledger one score_query per block of two or more items plus one item_feedback naming the singleton items (a count on a singleton is that item's outcome), so the base access rules apply. An optional declared partition per set bounds the leak in the lattice; a finer event is a recorded breach. audit_ledger is wrapped, not edited.

Boundaries

Deterministic channels only: a stochastic channel is a Markov kernel, for which the \(\mathrm{TV}\) contraction survives (_pushforward) but which has no partition, no lattice position, and no converse of Proposition 1; the Blackwell preorder on kernels is not treated. Items are independent and a patch changes its own item only, which the nearest-neighbour learner in output/eval_reuse_toys violated. Greedy ties break by index; \(\mathbb E[T]_{\text{static}}\) depends on that choice when two categories share a rate. The optimal adaptive policy is characterized for one hit (Theorem 10), two hits (Theorem 11) and one failure per category (Corollary to Lemma 12), and only computed beyond: Proposition 7 rules out index rules on the contexts it names, Lemma 9 and the survey rule out the obvious extremal rules, Corollary 14 rules out committing until a hit from three hits on, and the commit gap, the two-category case and the two-failure case rest on enumeration (sizes up to 24, 24 and 40 in one-off exact runs; the receipt runs smaller grids). The enumerations stop at six items for the lattice. The complexity of PATCH for fixed \(H\ge3\) and for unbounded \(H\) is open. No model or loop evidence.
